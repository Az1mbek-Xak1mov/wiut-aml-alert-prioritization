Which AML alerts get escalated?

Automated monitoring raises anti-money-laundering alerts from a customer's transaction history; specialists dismiss most of them and escalate a few. We explored 7 million transactions behind 14,000 labeled alerts to learn what separates the two, and built a model that ranks new alerts by their probability of escalation.

Our approach

Each alert (signal_id) comes with a variable-length history of transactions from the 180 days before the alert date. The task is to rank alerts by their chance of escalation, measured by ROC-AUC. Our workflow:

  1. Explore the alerts and the relational transaction table: coverage, timing artifacts, amount distributions by type and direction, and how escalated alerts differ from dismissed ones.
  2. Summarise each history into one row: counts, amount statistics, recent-window activity, burst behaviour, amount histograms, and — the key idea from EDA — relative features that compare each transaction type with the same customer's own typical level.
  3. Validate honestly: the test alerts are a random sample from the same period as training (not a time split), so we used stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated over several seeds.
  4. Model with gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM), lightly blended with CatBoost and a regularised logistic regression.

The alert day dominates, but it is not what separates the classesOn average an alert's history ends with a burst of ~41 transactions on the alert day itself; the last transaction is typically seconds before midnight. Escalated alerts are slightly busier across the whole window, but their timing shape is the same as dismissed ones, and features built on the last few transactions or on recent-vs-baseline activity scored near chance (AUC ≈ 0.49 – 0.61). Timing is how alerts are triggered, not how they are judged.

Relative size is the signalRaw amount levels mostly measure how "big" a customer is — all eight direction×type averages are strongly correlated (ρ 0.5 – 0.84). The escalation signal lives in the differences: a customer whose cash deposits are large compared with their own bank transfers is almost three times as likely to be escalated (10% → 29%). Tree models are poor at learning differences of two columns, so we engineered them explicitly.

Conclusion

  1. Escalation is a weak, diffuse signal. 17.2% of alerts are escalated, steady over time; no single feature exceeds AUC 0.62, and our best model reaches ≈ 0.66 cross-validated.
  2. What matters is the shape of a customer's amounts, relative to themselves — large cash deposits and card spending relative to bank transfers raise escalation risk; large bank transfers on their own lower it.
  3. Tiny card transactions at the bottom of the size range double the escalation rate (16% → 30%+).
  4. Bursts before midnight are everywhere (99% of alerts, 8% of transactions) and mark how alerts are triggered — but timing, recency and pass-through features barely separate the classes.
  5. Modelling lesson: explicit difference features + feature selection beat throwing hundreds of raw aggregates at the model.
